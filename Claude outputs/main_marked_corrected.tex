\begin{abstract}
Two-dimensional (2D) material–based memristors promise ultimate vertical scaling for high-density in-memory computing, yet their hardware realization demands physics-based, SPICE-compatible compact models. Conventional bulk-oxide-based memristor models may not account for the atomic thickness and distinct switching mechanisms of 2D active layers. Here, we present a generalized compact model derived from the minimum-energy pathway of atomic rearrangement during resistive switching. Validated against extensive experimental data, our model reliably captures switching dynamics across monolayer-to-multilayer insulators, active and passive electrodes, lateral 2D geometries, conventional oxides, and bio-organic systems, while incorporating the intrinsic stochasticity of switching. Preliminary results also indicate the model's potential to capture multi-state switching behavior. When implemented in a commercial circuit simulator, the model achieves robust convergence in simulations of content-addressable memory cells, bridging the gap between atomistic switching physics and system-level neuromorphic design.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Nonvolatile resistive switching random access memory (RRAM), commonly known as memristors, combine memory and processing in one nanoscale device, retaining data without power. They enable ultra-dense, low-energy, fast non-volatile storage, overcoming Flash limitations \cite{6193402}. Crucially, they power neuromorphic and in-memory computing, mimicking synapses to cut the von Neumann bottleneck, accelerating AI while reducing energy consumption dramatically \cite{Yang2013Memristive}. Since the demonstration by Samsung \cite{1419232} and then by HP \cite{Strukov2008}, Metal-Insulator-Metal (MIM) architecture has been adopted in industry to realize memristance and transition metal oxides (TMO), e.g., HfO$_{2}$, TaO$_{x}$ or TiO$_{2}$, have been used as insulators.  Foundries such as TSMC integrate them with CMOS as embedded memory and applications have been demonstrated for computer vision and deep-learning applications\cite{TSMC}. In these devices, an applied electric field drives the migration of metal cations or oxygen vacancies between the electrodes, forming conductive filaments that provide a low-resistance path and switch the device from a high-resistance state (HRS) to a low-resistance state (LRS). Applying a reverse bias or a reset pulse ruptures the filaments and restores the HRS. However, filament formation is inherently stochastic and site-specific, giving rise to cycle-to-cycle and device-to-device variability and, consequently, unreliable memory states \cite{Roldan2023Variability}. Recent years have seen tremendous activities in replacing the TMO with 2D materials like hexagonal boron nitride\cite{Zhang2024Memristors}, MoS$_{2}$ \cite{MoS2} and graphene \cite{Standley}, which offer atomically thin channels, lower switching energy, better uniformity, flexibility and extreme scalability.  Machine Learning computation has been  demonstrated for wafer-scale cross-bar arrays \cite{Tang2022WaferScale} and BEOL compatibility is demonstrated with standard CMOS process \cite{Shi2018Electronic}.

Compact models turn device physics into accurate, SPICE-compatible analytical equations, letting designers predict performance, power, variability and reliability before fabrication. Without them, new technologies cannot enter design kits or be validated in circuits, which makes compact models the bridge from lab breakthrough to commercial product. Building on the seminal work from HP Labs \cite{Strukov2008}, several phenomenological compact models \cite{Pickett2009Switching,Kvatinsky2013TEAM} have been proposed for TMO-memristors, in which the active-layer resistance is represented as a series combination of two voltage-controlled resistors. Researchers at Stanford University \cite{Jiang} and elsewhere \cite{Yu,Guan2012,Huang2013,Li2014} subsequently developed physics-based models founded on conductive-filament growth and rupture, which also captured the inherent stochasticity of resistive switching. In contrast, theoretical studies of 2D memristors have so far been confined largely to atomistic investigations of the switching mechanisms, using computationally demanding ab initio simulations and molecular dynamics \cite{Mitra2024Atomistic}. Together with experimental reports \cite{atomrister,zhang2019electric}, these studies point to a diverse set of switching mechanisms. Intrinsic mechanisms include phase transition \cite{zhang2019electric}, interlayer bridge formation \cite{Ducry2022} and sulphur-atom displacement \cite{Mitra2021}, while extrinsic, electrode-dependent mechanisms include atom adsorption at defect sites \cite{Hus2021,Ge2018} and filament growth along grain boundaries \cite{Sangwan2015}. Consequently, modelling approaches developed for TMO memristors cannot be directly applied to their 2D counterparts. Particularly, in monolayer-thick devices \cite{Rajput2024DefectEngineered} the active layer cannot meaningfully be partitioned into two series resistive regions. Compact modelling of 2D memristors therefore calls for a fundamentally new generalized approach applicable to all these distinct switching mechanisms. 

Here, we present a physics-based compact model derived from the minimum-energy pathway (MEP) of atomic rearrangement. By mapping inter-electrode atomic structures to crystal graphs, state evolution tracks reaction coordinates governed by Arrhenius dynamics, combining Schottky emission, metallic conduction, and Gaussian stochasticity. Demonstrating exceptional generalizability, the model accurately captures switching dynamics across diverse 2D materials, conventional oxides, bio-organics, and multi-state configurations. Implemented in commercial circuit simulators, it achieves robust convergence in content-addressable memory (CAM) cells, directly bridging atomistic physics and neuromorphic design.


%\section{Energy landscape model}

A voltage-controlled memristive system \cite{Chua1976} is defined by
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    I &= G(w,V)\,V, \\
    \frac{dw}{dt} &= f(w,V),
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:memristive_system}
\end{equation}
where $V$ is the applied voltage, $I$ is the current, $G$ is the conductance, $w$ is an internal state variable and $f(w,V)$ governs its evolution. Equation~\eqref{eq:memristive_system} specifies only the mathematical nature of the system; it does not prescribe the physical meaning of $w$ or the form of $f(w,V)$. In device models, these are typically chosen to represent a particular switching mechanism. This is a challenge for atomically thin materials, where, as mentioned before, the switching physics varies across devices and material systems. To create a unified framework capable of capturing this diversity, our model generalizes the switching mechanism by directly parameterizing the underlying potential energy landscape. In any memristor, the atomic configurations between the two electrodes change upon application of a terminal voltage (electric field), evolving from a stable semiconducting configuration (initial OFF state) to a stable metallic configuration (final ON state). The minimum energy pathway (MEP) is the lowest-energy route between them. A reaction coordinate, which is generally normalized, is a one-dimensional variable, $d$, that tracks this evolution from initial to final configuration as shown in Fig. \ref{energy landscape}. In this MEP, the initial and final states must be separated by a finite energy barrier to ensure non-volatility of the memory. An external electric field modulates the energy barrier and enables bi-directional switching \cite{Ducry2022,Mitra2021,Guha}. For a compact representation of the atomic configuration, we use a crystal graph, from which the energy of the system can be calculated from the vertex and edges \cite{Zhao}. The memristive state variable $w$ in this case is the normalized reaction coordinate $d$.

To govern the transition across this energy landscape, we utilize continuous Arrhenius rate dynamics. By coupling the applied electric field to the activation energy barriers separating the energy basins, this graph-based energy-landscape approach provides a generalized physical model, agnostic to any specific atomic-scale switching mechanism.

    \caption{Graph abstraction and field-driven energy landscape of atomic switching. (a) Structural abstraction mapping a crystal lattice onto a topological graph representation. (b) Minimum energy pathway along the reaction coordinate showing the initial, saddle point, and final atomic configurations, defining activation barriers $E_{\text{SET}}$ and $E_{\text{RESET}}$. (c) Electric-field-induced barrier lowering, illustrating the modulation of activation energies ($\Delta E_{\text{SET}}$ and $\Delta E_{\text{RESET}}$) during switching.}
    \label{energy landscape}
\end{figure}

The SET process transitions the system from the high-resistance (OFF, $d = 0$) to the low-resistance (ON, $d = 1$) state as the state variable $d$ increases, whereas the reverse RESET process corresponds to $d$ decreasing.

Each direction crosses its own barrier on the energy landscape. We therefore assign separate activation energies, $E_{\text{SET}}$ and $E_{\text{RESET}}$, to the two pathways. This is a deliberate modelling choice. When the two energies are equal, the landscape is symmetric and the SET and RESET characteristics mirror each other. When they differ, the landscape is asymmetric, so the model can reproduce devices whose SET and RESET thresholds and speeds differ. $E_{\text{SET}}$ and $E_{\text{RESET}}$ are therefore tuning parameters that allow one formulation to cover both symmetric and asymmetric switching characteristics.
 The term $\alpha qV$ represents the effective barrier lowering (equivalent to $\Delta E_{\text{SET}}$ and $\Delta E_{\text{RESET}}$ in Fig.~\ref{energy landscape}) due to the applied electric field \cite{Russo,Ielmini}. SET and RESET are activated only when the applied bias ($V$) overcomes certain onset voltages: $v_{\text{on}}$ and $v_{\text{off}}$. Depending on the phase-transition mechanism, SET and RESET can have different microscopic origins. For example, in the ECM (Electrochemical metallization)  kinetics model \cite{Menzel}, $\alpha$ is the charge-transfer coefficient inside a barrier-lowering term that reduces the effective barrier. In principle, one should assign different values for $\alpha_{\text{SET}}$ and $\alpha_{\text{RESET}}$ for the two pathways. However, $\alpha$ is a fitting parameter rather than a directly measured quantity. Using separate values changes the SET and RESET characteristics only negligibly compared with using one common value \cite{Ielmini}. We therefore adopt a \textbf{single $\alpha$ for both processes} since it reduces the number of free parameters to be optimized against the experimental data.
\\The general definition of memristive system requires the derivative of the state variable $d(t)$ to be a function of the state variable $d$ itself and applied bias ($V$), which is accurately maintained in our model through the Arrhenius equation. The dependence of the derivative of the state variable on the state variable $d$ acts as window function, which constrains the state variable bounds $d \in [0,1]$.
The evolution of $d(t)$ during the SET process ($V > v_{\text{off}}$) can be written as
\begin{equation}
    \frac{dd(t)}{dt} = k_{attempt} \cdot \exp\left( -\frac{E_{SET} - \alpha qV}{k_B T} \right) \cdot \left(1 - d(t)\right)^{p_{SET}}
    \label{eq:set}
\end{equation}

and for the RESET process ($V < v_{\text{on}}$) as
\begin{equation}
    \frac{dd(t)}{dt} = -\,k_{attempt} \cdot \exp\left( -\frac{E_{RESET} - \alpha q|V|}{k_B T} \right) \cdot d(t)^{p_{RESET}}
    \label{eq:reset}
\end{equation}
\noindent where the exponents $p_{SET}$ and $p_{RESET}$ shape the window functions $(1-d)^{p_{SET}}$ and $d^{\,p_{RESET}}$, and $|V|$ is used in Eq.~\eqref{eq:reset} because $V<0$ during RESET.

Memristors are intrinsically stochastic nanoelectronic devices since the resistive switching happens due to atomic rearrangement under electric field, which is an electrochemical process \cite{Filatov,Lee}. In oxide-based filamentary memristors, switching between the low-resistance state (LRS) and high-resistance state (HRS) relies on the restoration and rupture of conductive filaments (CFs) composed of oxygen vacancies ($V_O\text{s}$) via $O^{2-}$ ion jumps across nearest-neighbor lattice sites—processes governed by both ion drift and diffusion that are inherently stochastic. For 2D materials, electrode-atom-adsorption in sulfur vacancies of MoS$_2$ is of a similar nature \cite{Tsai2017}. Even cycle-to-cycle variation is observed in MoTe$_2$ based phase change memory \cite{Hong}.  Because these random transitions directly modulate the memristance $R(t)$ and the current $I(t)$, their effect carries into memory and neuromorphic circuits as state-dependent variability in read currents, thresholds and switching times. A purely deterministic compact model cannot reproduce this behaviour, so the stochasticity must be built into the model itself. In our model we therefore combine the deterministic Arrhenius-driven evolution of $d$ with a Gaussian stochastic component as follows

\begin{equation}
\Delta d(t) = \left( \frac{dd(t)}{dt} \right) \Delta t + \frac{\sigma}{\eta} \xi \sqrt{\Delta t}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\sigma$ is the standard deviation that controls the magnitude of random fluctuations acting on the state variable, $\eta$ is effective viscosity that scales the response of the state variable to the stochastic force, and $\xi$ is the standard normal random variable $\xi \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$.

The total current $I_{total}$ flowing through the device is modeled as a weighted combination of the currents from the initial semiconducting phase to the final metallic phase, based on the state variable $d(t)$.

    \label{fig:fitted_model_1}
\end{figure}

For the initial phase, the current follows the thermionic emission model:
\begin{equation}
    I_{schottky} = \operatorname{sgn}(V)\, I_s \left( \exp\left( \frac{|V|}{n V_T} \right) - 1 \right)
    \label{eq:schottky}
\end{equation}
where $I_s$ is the saturation current, $n$ is the ideality factor ($n = n_{SET}$ for $V \ge 0$ and $n = n_{RESET}$ for $V < 0$), and $V_T$ is the thermal voltage.

For the final metallic phase, the current follows Ohm's Law:
\begin{equation}
    I_{met} = G_{metallic} \cdot V
\end{equation}
where $G_{metallic}$ represents the conductance of the metallic filament.

The total current is calculated by interpolating between the two phases using the state variable $d(t)$:
\begin{equation}
    I_{total} = (1 - d(t)) \cdot I_{schottky} + d(t) \cdot I_{met}
\end{equation}

Upon calibration with the experimental data, the maximum value of the current will be limited to the compliance current. The complete list of model parameters is given in Table S1 of the Supplementary Information.

%\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}

We first validate our model against experimental data from various \textit{vertical} 2D binary memristors from diverse research groups \cite{hou2024strain,atomrister,zhang2019electric,Sanchali,deng2022,jnohBN} as presented in Fig \ref{fig:fitted_model_1} (D1 to D7) \textcolor{red}{and also in Fig. S1}. Some of these devices use both active (metal) electrodes and some of them passive (graphene and graphite) electrodes, and various active-layer materials (MoS$_{2}$, h-BN, NbOCl$_{2}$) either in monolayer or multilayer form.  As mentioned before, their switching mechanisms are different.  In both D2 and D3, switching is due to a phase change of MoTe$_{2}$; however, in one case it is from H to T and in the other from H to H$_{d}$. Good agreement with the experimental data for all these cases shows the generality of our model and justifies the modeling approach based on the MEP. We further validate our model for lateral 2D device (D7), conventional TMO based device (D8) and even honey-molecule based bio-organic device (D9)  \cite{WSe2,Jiang2016,SUEOKA2022106622}. Such an agreement is not surprising since our model is based on energy-landscape of atomic rearrangement, agnostic to the actual switching mechanism. The fitted parameters are presented in Table S2. We further demonstrate the ability of our model to capture intrinsic stochastic nature of h-BN memristors \cite{Lee} in Fig.\ref{fig:stochastic}. We attempt to predict the cycle-to-cycle variability \cite{deng2022} for 50 cycles using our model and observe low cycle-to-cycle dispersion. The statistical variation of the SET and RESET voltages is also in close agreement with the experiments. For the SET and RESET operations, the reported mean ($\mu$), standard deviation ($\sigma$), and coefficient of variation ($C_{\text{V}}$) are $(2.955\text{ V}, 0.183\text{ V}, 6.21\%)$ and $(-3.732\text{ V}, 0.142\text{ V}, 3.81\%)$, respectively. 

Although developed for binary memory, we explore the possibility of extending it to multistate memristors, relevant for artificial neurons. We first fit the baseline characteristics of the h-BN based multi-state device \cite{Shi2018Electronic}; then a sequential shift in the switching thresholds (see Note S1 for details), as illustrated by the color gradient, captures the cycle-to-cycle variability induced by shifting energy barriers $E_{SET}$ and $E_{RESET}$, while keeping the compliance current strictly constant. However, this methodology requires further exploration to capture pulse-width-dependent potentiation and depression.

Finally, we implement our Verilog-A model in SmartSpice \cite{SilvacoSmartSpice} to simulate a 3T1R (three-transistor, one-RRAM) CAM cell \cite{Jiang}. The cell comprises an RRAM device (R1), a pMOS (P1), an nMOS (N1), and an nMOS match-line driver (NML). The gates and sources of N1 and P1 connect to (DLn, SLn) and (DLp, SLp), respectively, while R1 bridges the resistor-source-line (SLr) and the shared drain node (NX). Biasing SLp, SLn, SLr, DLp, and DLn enables search operations and programming into logic `0', `1', or ``don't care'' (X) states (see Supplementary Information for detailed circuit logic).
